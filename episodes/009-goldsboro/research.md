### A. Department of Defense, "Narrative Summaries of Accidents Involving U.S. Nuclear Weapons 1950-1980" (current as of April 1981)
- Opened: https://archive.org/details/DODNarrativeSummariesofAccidentsInvolvingUSNuclearWeapons19501980 (PDF https://archive.org/download/DODNarrativeSummariesofAccidentsInvolvingUSNuclearWeapons19501980/DOD%20Narrative%20Summaries%20of%20Accidents%20Involving%20U.S.%20Nuclear%20Weapons%201950-1980.pdf ; OCR _djvu.txt). Item license: CC0; DoD work.
- Cover (p. i): "Attached are unclassified summaries describing the circumstances surrounding 32 accidents involving nuclear weapons." "There never has been even a partial inadvertant U.S. nuclear detonation despite the very severe stresses imposed upon the weapons involved in these accidents." "Only two accidents, those at Palomares and Thule, resulted in a widespread dispersal of nuclear materials." "Most of the aircraft accidents represented here occurred during logistic/ferry missions or airborne alert flights by Strategic Air Command (SAC) aircraft. Airborne alert was terminated in 1968 because of: -- Accidents, particularly those at Palomares and Thule, -- The rising cost of maintaining a portion of the SAC bomber force constantly on airborne alert, and, -- The advent of a responsive and survivable intercontinental ballistic missile force which relieved the manned bomber force of a part of its more time-sensitive responsibilities."
- Cover: "Since the location of a nuclear weapon is classified defense information, it is Department of Defense policy normally neither to confirm nor deny the presence of nuclear weapons at any specific place. In the case of an accident involving nuclear weapons, their presence may or may not be divulged at the time depending upon the possibility of public hazard or alarm."
- p. ii: compiled by DOD/DOE researchers "during December 1980-January 1981"; "Current as of April, 1981".
- p. 21 (PDF p. 24): "January 24, 1961 / B-52 / Goldsboro, North Carolina — During a B-52 airborne alert mission structural failure of the right wing resulted in two weapons separating from the aircraft during aircraft breakup at 2,000 - 10,000 feet altitude. One bomb parachute deployed and the weapon received little impact damage. The other bomb fell free and broke apart upon impact. No explosion occurred. Five of the eight crew members survived. A portion of one weapon, containing uranium, could not be recovered despite excavation in the waterlogged farmland to a depth of 50 feet. The Air Force subsequently purchased an easement requiring permission for anyone to dig there. There is no detectable radiation and no hazard in the area."
- (→ "Twenty years later" = April 1981 vs January 1961.)

### B. National Security Archive, EBB 475, "New Details on the 1961 Goldsboro Nuclear Accident" (William Burr, June 9, 2014; updated June 11, 2014)
- Opened (web reading): https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/nukevault/ebb475/
- Sandia report: "Analysis of the Safety Aspects of the MK 39 MOD 2 Bombs Involved in B-52G Crash Near Greensboro, North Carolina," SCDR 8-81, February 1961, J. M. de Montmollin and W. R. Hoagland (title says Greensboro). Doc 5 PDF (scan): https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/nukevault/ebb475/docs/doc%205%20AEC%20report%20Goldsboro%20accident.pdf
- Both multi-megaton Mk 39 bombs were in the "safe" position, but the crash initiated the fuzing sequence on both.
- Weapon 1: the crash pulled the safing pins from the Bisch generator (which supplied electric power to the weapon), and lanyards pulled safing pins from the weapon; the T-249 Arm/Safe switch "worked exactly as it was supposed to," preventing a nuclear explosion. Correction note: it was "Weapon No. 1" that was "the truly close call" (point raised by Eric Schlosser).
- Weapon 2: landed in free-fall; without the parachute the timer did not start the high-voltage battery ("trajectory arming" did not occur); the crash rotated the Arm/Safe switch's indicator drum to "armed"; the shock damaged the switch contacts; not close to detonation.
- ALT 197 "was already underway to remove" the lanyards; analysts recommended rapid implementation for the "MK 15/39 family" used in airborne alert.
- McNamara: "by the slightest margin of chance, literally the failure of two wires to cross, a nuclear explosion was averted."
- 1958 JCS documents: risk of inadvertent detonation of sealed-pit weapons a "negligible factor" (doc 1A/1B, RG 218).
- 1958 SAC letter: a "point of balance" between safety and weapon reliability and reaction time.
- 1958 Sidi Slimane, Morocco mishap prompted concern about future accidents as Western Europe became saturated with nuclear stockpiles.
- 1959 Sandia study (Carl Carlson): safety "fundamentally a matter of playing percentages"; estimated risk of spontaneous detonation 10^-8 (one in a hundred million).
- Late-1960s policy review: annual risk of detonation no greater than one in a million for a stockpile of ten thousand or more weapons.
- 1987 Sandia history (R. N. Brodie, SAND86-2955): the U.S. could not claim in an absolute sense that it takes every action to ensure weapon safety while weapons stay fully assembled on combat-ready systems (Archive's summary; script paraphrases with attribution "concluded").
- Palomares (Jan. 1966): B-52/KC-135 collision; seven crew died; HE in weapons exploded; plutonium contamination; one weapon lost at sea until recovered by divers. Thule (Jan. 1968): B-52 crashed on ice-covered bay; four weapons broke up and scattered radioactive debris; one crew member died.

### C. National Archives, "Brush with Catastrophe: The Day the U.S. Almost Nuked Itself" (Pieces of History blog, Jan. 22, 2021; Michael J. Hancock, archives technician, NARA College Park)
- Opened: https://prologue.blogs.archives.gov/2021/01/22/brush-with-catastrophe-the-day-the-u-s-almost-nuked-itself
- Bomber "Keep 19," B-52G of the 4241st Strategic Wing, 24-hour airborne alert off the Atlantic Coast; "only three days after President John F. Kennedy took office" (Kennedy inaugurated Jan. 20, 1961).
- During in-flight refueling the crew found the right wing leaking fuel; "more than 5,400 gallons (37,000 pounds)" lost "in less than three minutes"; directed to Seymour Johnson AFB for an emergency landing.
- Maj. Walter S. Tulloch ordered the crew to abandon; crew incl. Capt. Richard W. Hardin, 1st Lt. Adam C. Mattocks; one crewman "climbed out through the top hatch" (Mattocks per Wikipedia/Air & Space Forces); three died: "Majors Shelton and Richards and Sergeant Barnish."
- Wreckage covered "a roughly two square mile area."
- Mark 39: "two-stage, radiation-implosion thermonuclear bomb," produced 1957–1959, "more than 700 built"; 6,500–6,750 lb; about 12 ft long; yield 3–4 megatons (3.8 MT), "250 times the explosive power of the bomb dropped on Hiroshima."
- Parachute bomb found "hanging from a tree," "essentially undamaged." Free-fall bomb buried in a muddy field.
- "After eight days," the ordnance team had recovered most of the buried bomb, including "the 92 detonators"; the pit was recovered on January 29; the secondary was never found.
- "Broken Arrow," the military code name for accidents involving nuclear weapons.
- "Official statements indicate that there was no danger of the bombs exploding."
- Jones document caption: Parker F. Jones, Supervisor of the Nuclear Weapons Safety Department at Sandia, responding to a passage in Dr. Ralph Lapp's book describing the 1961 accident (Records of the JCS, National Archives).
